How to read a fan chart

No. 1
The root, in the center medallion. Here, Victoria herself.
2n
Double a person’s number for their father. Victoria’s father is No. 2.
2n+1
Double it and add one for their mother. Victoria’s mother is No. 3.
●
A filled dot marks pedigree collapse: one ancestor filling more than one slot, usually because cousins married. Eight people do that in this chart.

Questions Four we hear first

Before you drop a file.

Anything else, write to support@gildroot.com.

How do I get my family tree out of Ancestry, FamilySearch or MyHeritage?

Ancestry and MyHeritage both let you export a GEDCOM file from their desktop sites, usually from an “Export tree” option in your tree’s settings. FamilySearch doesn’t offer a direct export for a full pedigree; the usual route is a free program such as RootsMagic Essentials or Ancestral Quest Basics, which reads your FamilySearch tree and saves a GEDCOM.

Is anything about my family uploaded?

No. Your file is read on this computer, and the chart is drawn on this computer. The only things that leave it are a license key check when you buy, and anonymous counts like “7 generations” for our own analytics — never names, places or dates. You can turn off wifi after the page loads and keep working.

What if my tree has gaps, or I only know a few names?

A sparse tree is treated as deliberate, not broken: empty branches show as faint, unlabeled wedges, and you can trim them. Without a file at all, the Quick Builder lets you type in up to 15 people, and an Ask-the-family link lets a relative fill in the rest from their own phone.

Can it print names in Chinese, Hebrew, Arabic or other scripts?

Names in the Latin, Greek and Cyrillic alphabets print correctly, including accented letters like Seán, Dvořák and Ødegård. Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Hebrew and Arabic script can’t be printed yet. Before you pay, Gildroot shows you exactly which names those are and lets you enter a romanized form instead.
