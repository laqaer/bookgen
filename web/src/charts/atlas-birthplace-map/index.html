How Atlas works

Atlas reads each person’s birthplace from your file and works out which present-day country it is in. “Tábor, Bohemia, Austrian Empire” becomes Czechia. “Castlebar, County Mayo” becomes Ireland. “NY” becomes the United States. The engine knows more than 2,500 place names: countries in English and in their own languages, historical states, US states and their abbreviations, Canadian provinces, the four UK nations, and Irish counties.

The most common country gets the first of seven colors, the next the second, and so on. Everything after the seventh shares one gray, labeled “Other.” People with no birthplace keep the style’s own tone.

Some old places no longer match one country. Galicia is now split between Poland and Ukraine, and there is also a Galicia in Spain. Places like that are never guessed. The studio lists them, most common first, and you choose the country for each in two clicks.

You can also show US states separately, for a family that moved within the United States.

Who it suits

Families who came from several countries
The sample family’s chart shows seven countries in five generations. Each branch is its own color.
Immigrant stories
The generation where the color changes is the generation that moved.
Anyone checking their places
The studio says how many birthplaces it could place, and lists the rest. The free GEDCOM viewer shows the same for your whole file.

Charts and sizes

Atlas is a color mode, not a separate chart. It works on the fan, the bowtie and the pedigree, in all six styles, at every size.

The free Letter PDF comes in Ivory’s own tones. Atlas colors in a PDF are part of the $29 license; on screen they are free.

How to read it

Reading key

Color
The country the person was born in, as it is today.
Legend
Each country with its share: “Portugal 47%” means 47 of every 100 placed birthplaces on the chart.
Gray
“Other”: countries beyond the seven most common.
Plain
No birthplace in the file, so no color.
Summary
One line under the legend, such as “Born in seven countries across five generations.”
