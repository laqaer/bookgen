What is a fan chart?

A fan chart is a family tree drawn as a half circle, or more, with one person at the center and their ancestors in rings around them.

By the Gildroot team. Updated 30 September 2026.

How it is laid out

The person the chart is about sits in the center. Around them:

  • the first ring holds their 2 parents,
  • the second ring their 4 grandparents,
  • the third ring their 8 great-grandparents,
  • and every ring after that holds twice as many as the one inside it.

Each person sits directly outside their own child, so you can follow any line from the center outward, like a spoke. The father’s side takes the left half of the fan and the mother’s side the right.

That doubling is why the chart is a fan. A straight family tree runs out of paper sideways after four or five generations; the outer rings of a circle are long enough to hold many more names.

Half, three-quarter or full circle

Fan charts come in different sweeps. Gildroot draws three:

  • 180°, a half circle, printed landscape. The default for 5 generations or fewer.
  • 270°, three-quarters of a circle, with the title in the open space at the bottom. The default for 6 to 8 generations.
  • 360°, a full circle.

The numbering behind it

Genealogists number the places on a fan with a system called Ahnentafel, German for “ancestor table.” The person in the center is No. 1. For anyone numbered n, their father is 2n and their mother 2n + 1. In Queen Victoria’s chart, from Wikidata:

No.WhoRelationship
1VictoriaThe center
2Prince Edward, Duke of Kent and StrathearnFather (2 × 1)
3Princess Victoria of Saxe-Coburg-SaalfeldMother (2 × 1 + 1)
4George III of Great BritainFather’s father
5Charlotte of Mecklenburg-StrelitzFather’s mother

Every generation starts at a power of two: parents are 2–3, grandparents 4–7, great-grandparents 8–15. You never need these numbers to read a fan, but they explain why it fills the way it does.

When the same ancestor appears twice

Sometimes one person fills two or more places on a fan. It happens when the chart’s subject descends from that ancestor by two different lines, usually because two cousins married, often generations back. Genealogists call it pedigree collapse.

Gildroot marks each of those people with a small numbered circle and explains it in a legend (“appears 2 times”). In the fictional sample family, António Pacheco and Isabel Furtado appear twice because two of their great-grandchildren, second cousins, married in 1899. In Queen Victoria’s 8-generation chart, 14 people or couples appear more than once.

When to choose a fan chart

  • You want the most generations on one sheet. A fan fits 8 generations, 255 names, on a 24×36 poster. A pedigree chart tops out at 6.
  • You want to see the shape of your research. Full wedges show lines you have traced; empty ones show where to look next.
  • You want something to frame. A fan is round and balanced, and reads as a picture from across a room.

Choose a pedigree chart instead when full place names matter more than the number of generations, and a bowtie chart when the chart is about a couple.
