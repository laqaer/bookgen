Guide

Bring the family tree to Thanksgiving.

Thanksgiving is Thursday, 26 November 2026. It may be the one day this year the oldest and youngest of your family sit at the same table. Here is how to come home with more of your family tree than you left with.

By the Gildroot team. Updated 30 September 2026.

Two weeks before: send an Ask-the-family link

Start a chart in the studio with what you already know. You don’t need a family tree file: the Quick Builder takes up to 15 people, from you back to your great-grandparents, with a name, a birth year (or “about”) and a birthplace for each.

Then tap Ask the family. The studio makes a link that carries your partial tree inside it. Send it to an aunt, a cousin or a grandparent by text or email. They open it on their phone, see the blanks, fill in what they know, and tap Send back. When their link comes back to you, you merge it in one tap, and anything that disagrees with what you had is highlighted for you to choose.

The answers travel in the link itself, in the part of the address that is never sent to a website. Nothing about your family passes through Gildroot.

A tip from the studio

If the chart is going to be a Christmas gift, ask the relative to keep it a surprise.

A few days before: print a chart for the table

Print what you have on Letter paper. At 4 generations or fewer, Gildroot sets the chart as a keepsake with large type, full dates and birthplaces, which is easy for everyone to read over a coffee cup. Empty places show as faint wedges: an invitation to fill them in.

Bring a pen and a few spare copies. Relatives will write on it, and the notes in the margin are often the best part.

On the day: ask, listen, write it down

Pick a quiet moment, after the meal rather than during it. Sit with one person at a time, with the chart between you. Point at a name and ask what they remember. Write down names exactly as they spell them, and ask for places as precisely as they know them: the town, not only the country.

If you want to record the conversation on your phone, ask first. Some people talk more freely with the phone face down.

Questions to ask

Start with the easy ones. Nobody needs to answer all of them.

About them

  1. What is your full name, and who were you named after?
  2. Where and when were you born? What was the house like?
  3. What did your parents do for work?
  4. Where did you go to school? What was the walk there like?
  5. How did you meet your husband, wife or partner?
  6. What was your first job?

About their parents and grandparents

  1. What were your parents’ full names, including your mother’s maiden name?
  2. When and where were they born?
  3. What do you remember about your grandparents? Did you know all four?
  4. Where did your grandparents live? Did they come from another country?
  5. Were there any brothers or sisters who died young, or who moved away and lost touch?
  6. Did anyone in the family change their name, or spell it differently?

About places and moves

  1. Which town did the family come from, before here?
  2. Do you know why they moved, and roughly when?
  3. Did anyone keep letters, a family Bible, or papers from that time?
  4. Are there any places the family still talks about?

About the family’s stories

  1. What traditions did your family have at Thanksgiving, or other holidays?
  2. What was a typical meal in your house growing up?
  3. Who was the storyteller in the family? What stories did they tell?
  4. Is there a family recipe, and whose was it?
  5. Is there an object that has been handed down? Who had it before?
  6. What do you wish you had asked your own grandparents?
  7. Who else should I talk to?
  8. Is there anything you would like the grandchildren to know?

Some memories are painful, and some families have stories that are private. Let people skip a question without explaining why.

Afterwards: add it to the chart

Add the new names, years and places in the studio the same weekend, while the notes still make sense. If a relative wants to add more later, send them another Ask-the-family link.

By Christmas, the chart you started in November can be a framed gift. See a family tree for Christmas, or the print planner for sizes.
